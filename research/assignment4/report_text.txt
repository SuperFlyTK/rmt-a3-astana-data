Peak and Off Peak Differences Between Google and ORS Travel Time Estimates on Astana Routes

Public research folder: Browse the research files

Public source records: Collection records

Research question: How do Google traffic-aware travel-time estimates differ from ORS control estimates on fixed Astana routes during peak and off-peak periods?

Results and Discussion only: 2192 words; 6 figures/graphs; 10 tables. Table text, captions, headings and evidence-link lines are excluded from the word count. Conclusion: 374 words.

Abstract

Navigation services can return different travel times for the same endpoints. We compared Google traffic-aware estimates with openrouteservice (ORS), which supplied a control without live traffic, on 20 directed Astana routes. Archived evidence covers two dates and four planned comparison windows, with 80 valid pairs. The median of window median absolute differences was 679.78 seconds during peak periods and 408.60 seconds off peak, a descriptive gap of 271.18 seconds. All 572 archived ORS observations passed the recorded checks, but ORS durations stayed unchanged within each route. The original scheduled collection target was missed. Individual Google estimates were not retained, preventing route-level reanalysis and significance testing. These findings describe disagreement for the sampled routes and times; they establish neither prediction accuracy nor a causal traffic effect.

1 Introduction

1 1 Background and problem context

Drivers and transport researchers use navigation estimates to judge journey duration. Two services can recommend different streets, assign different speeds to the same streets, or react differently to departure conditions. A difference between their estimates therefore needs explanation before either estimate is treated as a reliable description of a trip in Astana.

Three concepts guide this study. Agreement describes how close two services are to each other. Variability describes how their difference changes between sampled routes or times. Accuracy concerns the difference between a prediction and an independently observed journey. We collected service outputs without observing car journeys, so the evidence supports agreement analysis.

1 2 Literature review and disagreement

Derrow-Pinion et al. (2021) describe a Google Maps prediction system that uses road structure and traffic information. Soon et al. (2023) review traffic forecasting and show why temporal coverage, input quality and unusual events matter. These papers explain the value of matching endpoints and departure conditions. They do not establish the internal design of the services used in our present comparison.

Fu et al. (2023) compare routing methods for common origin-destination pairs. Verma et al. (2025) show that strong statistical fit between routing outputs can coexist with differences in average duration. Boeing and Zhou (2026) develop a model for minimally congested travel using Google estimates as its validation target. Together, these studies support examining differences in time units and stating clearly what the reference output represents.

The literature also contains a meaningful divergence in reported agreement. Elnaggar et al. (2025) report close agreement in a comparison involving Google navigation estimates, whereas Bastarianto et al. (2025) and Valero et al. (2025) find mismatches between API estimates and reported journeys. The comparison targets differ: agreement between service outputs does not establish agreement with a traveller's experience, and self-reported journeys introduce recall and rounding uncertainty. Our evidence can examine the first issue but cannot resolve the second.

Mansurova et al. (2025) provide empirical bus operations data from Astana, separating running and stopping behaviour. This establishes useful local context, but bus operations cannot validate private-car estimates directly. Hodson (2022) explains why absolute and squared differences have different purposes. We use the median to describe a typical gap and squared differences to make larger gaps visible, without calling either an error against observed travel.

The Assignment 2 review retained ten scholarly sources within its declared five-year eligibility window, from 3 October 2021 to the search date of 3 October 2026. The Assignment 2 literature did not resolve how traffic-aware and open-routing control estimates differ on these Astana routes across the predefined periods. A local comparison can document that disagreement, but it cannot separate traffic inputs from provider-specific routing and speed assumptions. The initial provider designs were constrained by access and publication permissions, leading to the dated Google-ORS amendment recorded in Assignment 3.

2 4 Metrics and interpretation rules

For a retained pair, let G and O denote Google and ORS durations in seconds. The signed difference is d=G-O, and the absolute difference is |d|. A positive signed difference means Google returned a longer estimate. MAE is the mean of |d|; RMSE is the square root of the mean of d squared. The mean symmetric absolute percentage difference is the mean of 100|G-O|/((G+O)/2). The IQR is the 75th percentile minus the 25th percentile, using linearly interpolated quartiles.

The period summary is the median of its recorded window medians. It is not the median of all individual pairs, which cannot be reconstructed from the archived Google evidence. The peak-off-peak contrast subtracts the off-peak summary from the peak summary; their ratio divides peak by off peak. Length comparability requires 100|LG-LO|/((LG+LO)/2) at most five percent. Similar length cannot establish that two routes use the same streets.

Reported time summaries are rounded to two decimal places, while the evidence files retain their original precision. Period contrasts are calculated before rounding the displayed period values.

The original route-by-day test and confidence intervals are withheld because the retained window summaries do not recover paired route-level differences. We report descriptive contrasts and show how the result changes when a window is omitted. No trained model, training-test split or causal treatment is used; the selected method remains Block E from Assignment 3.

Duplicate checking uses exact rows, repeated request identifiers and conflicting phase-cycle-route-provider identities; neither repeated equal durations at different timestamps nor reverse routes count as duplicate requests.

Next, we check that each Google batch agrees with its protocol and archived request-status counts. The exploratory cycle is displayed separately. The four registered follow-up batches all contain 20 valid pairs and satisfy the minimum of five pairs for a numeric summary. Their recorded maximum request-start gaps range from 0.000702 to 0.001219 seconds, comfortably within the 60-second pairing rule. These checks support the batch identities and timing; they cannot reconstruct missing individual Google values.

For interpretation, we first establish which rows and windows exist, then compare the magnitude and direction of the saved differences. We inspect route-length comparability before discussing a traffic-related explanation. Finally, we compare the observations with each original expectation, examine sensitivity and restrict the answer to the sampled routes and dates. This order prevents a clear-looking chart from being mistaken for evidence of accuracy or causation.

3 9 Additional interpretation and answer to the question

The strongest contrast is between the first peak window and the morning off-peak window: their median absolute gaps differ by 369.45 seconds. The broad period contrast remains positive when each window is omitted, yet its magnitude changes noticeably. This makes the direction more defensible within the observed sample than a claim that a specific ratio is a recurring property of Astana travel.

A non-finding concerns variability. The two peak signed IQRs are 218.30 and 230.23 seconds, below the off-peak values of 261.30 and 279.48 seconds. Thus larger central disagreement was not accompanied by a larger within-window signed spread in this amended comparison. This observation does not test original ER4 because that expectation concerns TomTom and 2GIS.

Provider-specific road speeds and route selection could produce the same broad pattern as different responses to congestion. The constant ORS estimates demonstrate that this control contributed no observed within-route temporal change. The lower length-comparability share in the first peak batch supplies a further route-choice explanation, but without retained Google geometry its contribution cannot be separated from other provider differences.

The scope is the fixed public-endpoint car routes and the recorded windows on the two study dates. The results do not cover buses, walking, every Astana district, weekday commuting across a longer period, or the original provider pair. Both dates fall on a weekend, which further limits any claim about typical working-day congestion.

The most useful surprise is the complete stability of ORS durations and distances within each route. This means that a large archive of requests can still contain little temporal information about one provider. It also explains why record counts and the effective amount of independent comparison evidence must be reported separately.

Answer to the research question: on the sampled Astana routes and dates, Google-ORS median absolute disagreement was greater in the recorded peak windows, with a median-of-window-medians gap of 271.18 seconds relative to off peak (Figure 3), without establishing accuracy or a causal traffic effect.

4 Conclusion

Google and ORS disagreed more in the recorded peak windows than in the recorded off-peak windows on the sampled Astana routes. The median of window median absolute differences was 679.78 seconds at peak and 408.60 seconds off peak, giving a descriptive contrast of 271.18 seconds. This answers the amended research question for the observed routes and dates. It does not show that either service predicts actual driving time more accurately or that traffic alone caused the difference.

The contribution is a documented comparison with clear boundaries. All planned comparison windows are represented, the collection phases remain separate, and the central differences are accompanied by spread and route-length evidence. The larger peak disagreement is visible across the recorded summaries. Its exact size remains sensitive to which windows are retained, so the observed ratio should not become a general adjustment factor for navigation estimates in Astana.

The control result is especially important for understanding the evidence. ORS returned unchanged duration and distance values within every route. Repeating these requests produced more records but no observed temporal variation in the control estimate. The provider gap therefore combines the behaviour of a changing traffic-aware output with a stable comparison. Different routing choices and speed assumptions remain possible explanations alongside changing traffic conditions.

The study also leaves original commitments unresolved. The earlier provider comparison was not performed, the original automatic collection target was missed, and the original route-level inferential analysis could not be supported. The Google aggregates cannot be independently recalculated from saved individual responses. These are substantive limits on the claims available from the project, even though the archived ORS observations passed the recorded checks. A successful amended comparison does not erase the unmet objectives or test the original provider-specific expectations.

Future work should first obtain permission to retain auditable paired numerical outputs and both route geometries. It should then cover working days and weekends across a longer observation period, with repeated matched routes in each period. Independently timed car journeys on those same routes would make an accuracy assessment possible. Until such evidence exists, the present findings are useful as a local example of provider disagreement and as a warning that agreement with another routing service cannot replace validation against travel on the road.
